Q&A Panel Bank — 20 Questions with STARE Model Answers

These are the questions most likely to be posed by the external panel. Each model answer uses the STARE framework: State what you know → Tie to project → Acknowledge gap → Reason with theory → End with what you'd do.

DATAHow do you know your training data does not have survivorship bias?
S — State: "Our dataset was collected from [X system] over [Y period]. We checked the collection methodology and confirmed [specific mechanism]."

T — Tie: "In our IADS dataset, we verified that all 8,500 tracks include both successful detections AND near-miss events where the target evaded — so we are not only learning from cases where the system succeeded."

A — Acknowledge: "We cannot fully rule out selection bias in which engagements were included in the training archive — this is a standing limitation of historically-collected military datasets."

R — Reason: "Survivorship bias would manifest as the model performing well on 'clean' canonical threats but failing on degraded-mode signatures, electronic countermeasure profiles, or low-observable variants. We tested this by holding out all ECM-environment tracks and checking performance degradation."

E — End: "To fully address this, we would need access to the raw sensor logs before any filtering was applied by the collection system — a request we have flagged for the data custodian."

METRICSYour classifier achieves 97.5% accuracy. What if an adversary simply saturates your system with 200 simultaneous tracks — can it maintain this?
Saturation is a latency problem, not an accuracy problem — and they require different answers.

S — "Our RF classifier at 97.5% accuracy has a measured inference time of 0.8ms per track on [target hardware]." T — "At 200 simultaneous tracks, total classification load is 200 × 0.8ms = 160ms — well within our 500ms tier-2 latency budget." A — "However, we did NOT test batch inference under memory contention on the actual IADS hardware — on an embedded system this could be 3–5× slower." R — "RF inference is embarrassingly parallel: 200 tracks can be distributed across [N] cores simultaneously. The bottleneck is not algorithm throughput but data ingestion from the radar processor." E — "We would benchmark on the target fire control computer with a synthetic 200-track injection to get measured latency under load before operational clearance."

ROBUSTNESSWhat happens to your model when the adversary uses a stealth aircraft with a radar cross-section 100× smaller than anything in your training data?
Out-of-distribution inputs are the single most important deployment risk — and acknowledging this directly impresses panels.

S — "Our training distribution has RCS values ranging from [min] to [max] m². An RCS 100× smaller would be [value] m² — well below our training minimum." A — "This is a genuine vulnerability: a discriminative classifier (RF, SVM, DNN) will extrapolate outside training distribution in unpredictable ways. It may classify the stealth aircraft as whichever training class has the nearest feature vector — likely a bird or weather return." R — "The principled detection approach for novel signatures is Isolation Forest (L5): a track with RCS 100× smaller would be an extreme outlier in the training distribution, producing high anomaly score regardless of class. We would recommend: classifier handles known threats → IF flags novel signatures → analyst escalates." E — "Ideally, we would include simulated low-observable signatures (via RCS modelling tools) in training data, even without class labels, to improve the classifier's coverage of the stealth regime."

RLYour DQN agent achieved 93% win rate against random OPFOR. What happens in exercise 3, when OPFOR has watched your agent play twice and starts exploiting its patterns?
Fixed trained policies are the Achilles heel of RL deployment — any strong answer acknowledges this directly.

S — "A trained DQN policy is a fixed function π(a|s;θ) — the weights θ do not update during deployment." A — "An experienced OPFOR who has observed the agent across two exercises will identify its tactical patterns — e.g., the agent always flanks left when OPFOR holds the northern objective. This is exploitable." R — "The adversarial robustness problem in RL: fixed policies that are near-optimal against naive opponents may be brittle against adaptive ones. Published literature (self-play, PSRO — Policy Space Response Oracles) addresses this: train the agent against a diverse mixture of opponent strategies, including past versions of itself." E — "For our project, we would implement a periodic retraining cycle: after each exercise, analyse which OPFOR tactics achieved highest win rate against our agent → include those tactics in the OPFOR training pool → retrain. This never-ending arms race is the operational reality of AI in contested environments."

ETHICSIf your autonomous recommendation system recommends engaging a target that turns out to be a medical helicopter, who is legally responsible?
This question has one correct answer: the human decision-maker who authorised the engagement is responsible. The system is a tool, not an actor.

S — "Our system operates at Level 2 autonomy (human on the loop). A human operator reviewed and confirmed the engagement recommendation before execution." T — "Our system provides: the threat classification with SHAP explanation, the Q-value ranking of options, and an IFF status indicator. The operator is presented with all information the system has." R — "Under IHL: the combatant who executes an engagement is responsible for applying the principles of distinction, proportionality, and precaution. An AI system that presents a recommendation is analogous to a targeting radar — it is a tool, not an agent with legal personality. The commander who acts on the recommendation bears responsibility." A — "That said, there is a shared responsibility dimension: if the system's classifier had systematic false-positive errors for medical helicopters that were known to the developers but not disclosed, there may be institutional liability for the system developer (analogous to a defective weapon manufacturer's liability)." E — "The correct design response: add a positive IFF check as a mandatory pre-requisite for engagement recommendation output — the system should never recommend engaging an asset that has not failed IFF. This is a ROE-level constraint, not an algorithm-level one."

DEPLOYMENTWhat is your estimate of the sim-to-real performance gap for your project, and how did you arrive at that estimate?
Every project has a sim-to-real gap. A student who says "none" fails the panel; one who quantifies it with a principled estimate passes.

S — "Our simulation accuracy/success rate is [X%]. We estimated the real-world performance as approximately [X minus Y]% based on three gap factors." T/R — "Gap factor 1: SENSOR FIDELITY — our training radar data used a calibrated simulation; real radar has multi-path, clutter, and jamming that increases feature noise → estimated −3 to −5pp. Gap factor 2: OPFOR ADAPTATION — simulation OPFOR is scripted; real OPFOR adapts → estimated −5 to −8pp for RL/MARL projects. Gap factor 3: EDGE CASES — simulation cannot enumerate every operational scenario; tails of real operational distributions are underrepresented → estimated −3pp. Total estimated gap: −11 to −16 percentage points." "This is consistent with published evidence: L7 PPO drone achieved 94% simulation → 78% real (16pp gap); L8 MARL estimated ~65% real vs 82% simulation (17pp gap)." E — "Validation plan: staged testing — first in hardware-in-the-loop simulation with real sensor feeds, then restricted live field trial on a representative subset of scenarios before full operational clearance."

INTEGRATIONHow would your project integrate with JANUS or existing C2 systems in the Indian military? What are the data format and latency requirements?
System integration is where most academic projects fail — panels want to see you have thought beyond the algorithm.

S — "JANUS uses [Link-11/16 equivalent data format / scenario XML files / specific API]. Our system needs to ingest [feature list] at [frequency] and output [recommendation format]." T — "For our IADS classifier: input is radar track data (range, azimuth, elevation, RCS, velocity, Doppler) at 1-second update intervals. Output: 5-class probability vector + SHAP explanation. This maps directly to the JANUS track object format with an added AI-assessment field." R — "Integration layers required: (1) Data adapter: JANUS track format → normalised feature vector for our RF model. (2) Inference API: REST or gRPC endpoint on fire control computer. (3) Output injector: model output → JANUS AI-advisory field → operator display overlay." A — "We have not actually tested with live JANUS data — we used a [simulated/synthetic] representation. True integration would require a JANUS API access agreement and a dedicated integration sprint." E — "We estimate 2–3 months of integration engineering to move from trained model to JANUS-embedded advisory system, following the standard defence software integration V&V process."

CLUSTERINGYour OPFOR clustering produced 5 archetypes. A field commander asks: 'What do I actually DO with this?' Give a 60-second operational briefing.
The answer to 'so what?' is the most important question any project must answer. Prepare this for every project.

"Commander, we analysed 8,000 historical OPFOR patrol trajectories and discovered 5 distinct operational archetypes. Here is what this means for your operations: [1-sentence description of each archetype and its tactical signature]. Archetype 3 — INFILTRATION — is the critical finding: it matches 12.4% of tracks and was previously unclassified. These are small units moving during low-visibility, exploiting border-gap corridors between 0200 and 0400 hours. We recommend: (1) Shift ISR surge to the 0200–0400 window in sectors matching Archetype 3 corridors. (2) Brief your [unit type] to specifically look for the small-unit, high-route-variation signature. (3) Any new patrol trajectory that does not match one of these 5 archetypes will be automatically flagged by our anomaly detection system as Priority Intelligence Requirement — these are the potential novel tactics or adversary adaptation events you most need to know about. The system runs continuously on incoming trajectory data and updates your intelligence picture every 6 hours."

Project Topic Guides — T1 through T8

T1

Threat Classification System (L2)

Supervised Classification
Problem Statement

Build a multi-class classifier for air/sea/ground threat identification from sensor data. Choose a specific sensor modality and operational context.

Suggested Datasets

SRTM radar terrain dataset (public), synthetic radar track generator (JANUS outputs), NSL-KDD for cyber analogue, or design your own synthetic dataset using realistic IADS parameters from the course.

Required Algorithms

Primary: Random Forest (mandatory — establishes course benchmark). Compare against: kNN, SVM (RBF kernel), XGBoost. Address class imbalance with at least one technique (SMOTE or class weighting).

Key Metrics to Report

Per-class RecallAUROC per classConfusion MatrixSHAP Feature ImportanceInference Latency (ms)

Benchmark to Beat

Course L2 IADS result: RF 97.5%, AUC=0.991 on 8,500 samples with 11 features.

Panel Questions to Prepare For

1. "What is your per-class recall for the rarest class? What is the operational consequence of missing it?"

2. "How would you retrain when the adversary fields a new threat type with only 20 labelled examples?"

3. "Your SHAP analysis shows feature X dominates classification. What if the adversary deliberately manipulates feature X?"

T2

Mission Planning Regression (L3)

Supervised Regression + Sensor Fusion
Problem Statement

Predict a continuous mission outcome: fuel consumption, mission duration, munitions required, or casualty estimate. Integrate at least two sensor/intelligence data streams via a fusion approach.

Required Elements

Regression chain: OLS → Ridge/Lasso (compare L1 vs L2 penalty) → XGBoost. Kalman Filter for any sequential sensor fusion component. RMSE and R² as primary metrics. Residual analysis (are errors random or structured?).

Key Metrics

R² ScoreRMSEResidual PlotFeature ImportanceKalman Gain Analysis

Benchmark

Course L3 XGBoost: R²=0.912, RMSE=2.06 hours on mission duration. Kalman+RF fusion: 96.2% TPR.

T3

OPFOR Pattern Intelligence (L4)

Unsupervised Clustering + Visualisation
Problem Statement

Discover operational archetypes from movement, communications, or logistics data without prior labels. Identify novel or deceptive patterns.

Required Algorithms

k-Means (with Elbow + Silhouette for k selection), DBSCAN (ε and MinPts calibrated via k-distance plot), at least one of: GMM or Ward hierarchical. PCA or UMAP for visualisation. ARI for cross-method validation.

Key Metrics

Silhouette Score (target >0.65)ARI (cross-method)DBSCAN Noise %PCA Explained Variance

Unique Requirement

Must name each discovered cluster with an operational label and provide a 2-sentence operational briefing for each archetype (the "Commander, what do I DO with this?" slide).

T4

Cyber Defence Anomaly Detection (L5)

Unsupervised Anomaly Detection
Problem Statement

Build a tiered anomaly detection system for a military network or critical infrastructure. Detect known attacks AND novel zero-day signatures.

Required Architecture

Tier-1: Isolation Forest (real-time, <0.005ms). Tier-2: Autoencoder (deeper analysis, ~0.05ms). Optional Tier-3: LSTM Autoencoder for user behaviour sequences. Calculate daily compute load for a realistic packet volume. Test against at least one attack type NOT in training data.

Key Metrics

TPR (Recall)FPRAUROCDaily Compute (seconds)MTTD

Dataset Options

KDD Cup 1999 (public), CICIDS 2017/2018 (public), NSL-KDD (public — recommended for class balance), or synthetic generation from the L5 case study parameters.

T5

Wargame Decision Support (L6)

Q-Learning / DQN
Required Algorithm

Dueling + Double DQN with experience replay. Compare against: vanilla DQN (baseline 1), Q-Table (baseline 2). Implement reward shaping AND curriculum learning. Output: top-3 actions with Q-values and at least one SHAP attribution.

Environment Options

Custom Python gym environment (10×10 to 30×30 grid), OpenAI Gym MiniGrid (modified for military scenario), STARCRAFT II (advanced), or a custom JANUS-inspired hex grid.

Key Metrics

Win Rate vs RandomWin Rate vs Scripted OPFORWin Rate vs HumanTD Error CurveQ-Value Convergence

Policy Gradient — PPO / SAC
