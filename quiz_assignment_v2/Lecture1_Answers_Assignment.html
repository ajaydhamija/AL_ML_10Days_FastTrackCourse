Q1. In the kidney-stone study, treatment A succeeded in 81/87 small-stone and 192/263 large-stone cases. What is A's overall success rate?

  • A. 83%, the average of 93% and 73%
  • B. 93%, because A wins in every subgroup
  • C. 166%, the sum of the subgroup rates
  • D. 78%
Answer D. Totals come from counts: (81+192)/(87+263) = 273/350 = 78%. Averaging or adding subgroup percentages ignores group sizes and is meaningless.

Q2. Treatment A beats B for both small and large stones, yet loses overall (78% vs 83%). The best explanation is:

  • A. A was given mostly the harder large-stone cases, so its total is dragged down by case mix
  • B. Random chance
  • C. B is genuinely the better treatment
  • D. The data contain an arithmetic error
Answer A. Simpson's paradox: stone size is a confounder affecting both treatment choice and outcome. 75% of A's patients had large stones vs 23% of B's.

Q3. A p-value of 0.03 means:

  • A. There is a 3% chance that the null hypothesis is true
  • B. There is a 97% chance the effect is real
  • C. The effect is small
  • D. If the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would occur about 3% of the time
Answer D. A p-value is computed assuming H0 is true. It is not the probability that H0 is true, and says nothing about effect size.

Q4. A coating trial with 40 sorties per arm finds detection 65% vs 45% (p = 0.072). The correct conclusion is:

  • A. The coating has no effect
  • B. The result is inconclusive: the trial was under-powered for an effect of this size
  • C. The coating reduces detection by exactly 20 points
  • D. The coating increases detection
Answer B. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The 95% CI for the difference is about -1 to +41 points; about 93 sorties per arm were needed for 80% power.

Q5. Twenty independent tactics are each tested at alpha = 0.05 and none truly has an effect. The probability that at least one appears significant is about:

  • A. 5%
  • B. 20%
  • C. 64%
  • D. 100%
Answer C. \(1-(1-0.05)^{20}=1-0.95^{20}\approx0.64\). Correct with Bonferroni (alpha/m) and confirm on fresh data.

Q6. One million simulated engagements per tactic give hit rates 50.0% vs 50.2% with p = 0.005. This shows:

  • A. A statistically significant but operationally trivial difference
  • B. A large, important improvement
  • C. An invalid test
  • D. That more data always reduces bias
Answer A. With huge samples tiny effects become significant. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals and judge operational relevance.

Q7. Anscombe's quartet teaches that:

  • A. Correlation always implies causation
  • B. Outliers never affect correlation
  • C. Datasets with identical means, variances, correlation (0.816) and regression line can look completely different, so always plot the data
  • D. r = 0 means no relationship
Answer C. Summary statistics hide shape: a curve, an outlier or a single leverage point can produce the same r.

Q8. Units with the most maintenance hours have the most breakdowns. The most likely explanation is:

  • A. Maintenance causes breakdowns
  • B. Reverse causation: failing vehicles attract maintenance
  • C. A confounder: unit budget
  • D. Simpson's paradox
Answer B. The arrow runs from breakdowns to maintenance, not the other way.

Q9. Across 17 European countries stork pairs correlate with birth rates (r about 0.62). This is best explained by:

  • A. Storks deliver babies
  • B. A confounder such as land area or rural population
  • C. Measurement error
  • D. Regression to the mean
Answer B. A common cause drives both variables; the correlation is spurious.

Q10. Flight instructors notice that cadets praised after excellent landings usually do worse next time. This is mainly:

  • A. Evidence that praise harms performance
  • B. Survivorship bias
  • C. Regression to the mean
  • D. The base-rate fallacy
Answer C. An exceptional landing contains luck; the next one tends to be closer to the cadet's average regardless of feedback. Use a control group.

Q11. A detector has 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity; 1 in 1,000 tracks is a real threat. Roughly what fraction of alarms are real threats?

  • A. 99%
  • B. 50%
  • C. 9%
  • D. 1%
Answer C. PPV = (0.99 x 0.001) / (0.99 x 0.001 + 0.01 x 0.999) is about 0.09. About 10 true alarms and 100 false ones per 10,000 tracks.

Q12. Wald recommended armouring returning bombers where:

  • A. Bullet holes were densest
  • B. Uniformly over the whole aircraft
  • C. No bullet holes were seen, because aircraft hit there did not return
  • D. Only on the wings
Answer C. Survivorship bias: the missing aircraft were hit in the engines and cockpit. The absence of data is the signal.

Q13. What was the key analytical error in the Challenger O-ring discussion?

  • A. Examining mainly flights with damage, which hid the temperature trend shown by damage-free flights
  • B. Using too many data points
  • C. Using a logistic model
  • D. Ignoring the shuttle's weight
Answer A. Excluding no-event cases removes the contrast that reveals the trend; the launch also extrapolated far below any tested temperature.

Q14. A classifier separates wolves from huskies by detecting snow in the background. This is an example of:

  • A. Overfitting to label noise
  • B. Underfitting
  • C. Class imbalance
  • D. Shortcut learning: right answer for the wrong reason
Answer D. The model exploited a spurious cue correlated with the label (as Clever Hans did); explanation tools such as LIME exposed it.

Q15. A mine detector scores 94.1% in Afghanistan but 58.7% in Iraq. The best pre-deployment safeguard is:

  • A. Train a bigger network on more Afghan data
  • B. Increase the decision threshold
  • C. Report training accuracy only
  • D. Test on data from the deployment domain and monitor feature distributions (e.g. the KS statistic)
Answer D. Overfitting to the training domain plus distribution shift. More biased data amplifies bias; only deployment-domain testing reveals the gap.

Problem 1: Simpson's paradox in COA-tool evaluation 20 marks

Two course-of-action planning tools, X and Y, were used in 600 exercise engagements. Mission successes by threat level:

Threat levelTool XTool Y
Low45 / 50170 / 200
Medium60 / 8055 / 80
High70 / 1706 / 20

(a) Compute each tool's success rate in each threat level and overall. [6]
(b) Explain precisely why the overall comparison misleads. [4]
(c) Compute directly standardised success rates using the combined threat mix of all 600 engagements as the standard population. [6]
(d) Write a two-sentence recommendation to the programme manager. [4]

Show full solution

(a) X: low 90.0%, medium 75.0%, high 41.2%; overall 175/300 = 58.3%. Y: low 85.0%, medium 68.75%, high 30.0%; overall 231/300 = 77.0%. X is better in every stratum (+5.0, +6.25, +11.2 points) yet 18.7 points worse overall.

(b) Threat level is a confounder: it affects success and was distributed very differently. 170/300 = 57% of X's engagements were high-threat, against 20/300 = 7% of Y's. The overall rate is a weighted average with weights equal to each tool's own threat mix, so X is penalised for its harder assignments (Simpson's paradox).

(c) Combined mix: low 250, medium 160, high 190 (total 600), so weights 0.4167, 0.2667, 0.3167. X: 0.9(0.4167) + 0.75(0.2667) + 0.4118(0.3167) = 0.375 + 0.200 + 0.130 = 70.5%. Y: 0.85(0.4167) + 0.6875(0.2667) + 0.30(0.3167) = 0.354 + 0.183 + 0.095 = 63.3%. On a common case mix X outperforms Y by about 7 points.

(d) Tool X performs better than Y at every threat level and by about 7 points on a standardised mix; its lower raw rate reflects its assignment to high-threat sectors. Retain X, and in future trials randomise tool assignment across threat levels so that raw comparisons are fair.

Problem 2: Significance, power and multiple testing 20 marks

A new jamming pod is trialled. Hostile radar achieved lock-on in 32 of 50 control sorties and 20 of 50 sorties with the pod.

(a) State $H_0$ and $H_1$ and carry out a two-sided two-proportion z-test at $\alpha=0.05$. [6]
(b) Compute a 95% confidence interval for the reduction (unpooled standard error) and interpret it. [4]
(c) The same programme tested 12 different pods and this was the best result. Re-assess significance with a Bonferroni correction. [4]
(d) How many sorties per arm would give 80% power to detect a reduction from 64% to 40%? [6]

Show full solution

(a) $H_0: p_c=p_j$; $H_1: p_c\ne p_j$. $\hat p_c=0.64$, $\hat p_j=0.40$; pooled $\hat p=52/100=0.52$. $SE=\sqrt{0.52\times0.48\times(1/50+1/50)}=\sqrt{0.009984}=0.0999$. $z=0.24/0.0999=2.40$; two-sided $p=2[1-\Phi(2.40)]=0.016<0.05$: reject $H_0$ at the 5% level.

(b) $SE_u=\sqrt{0.64(0.36)/50+0.40(0.60)/50}=\sqrt{0.009408}=0.0970$. 95% CI $=0.24\pm1.96(0.097)=(0.050,\,0.430)$. The pod reduces lock-on probability by somewhere between 5 and 43 points: positive, but very imprecise.

(c) With 12 tests, the Bonferroni per-test threshold is $0.05/12=0.0042$. Since $p=0.016>0.0042$, the result is not significant after correction. Without correction the chance of at least one false positive among 12 null pods is $1-0.95^{12}=46\%$. The finding should be treated as a hypothesis and confirmed in a fresh, pre-registered trial.

(d) $n\approx\dfrac{(1.96+0.84)^2[0.64(0.36)+0.40(0.60)]}{0.24^2}=\dfrac{7.84\times0.4704}{0.0576}=64.0$, so about 64 sorties per arm (more if a Bonferroni-corrected $\alpha$ is used).

Problem 3: Base rates and two-stage screening for insider threats 20 marks

An organisation has 50,000 accounts, of which 2 in every 10,000 are compromised on a given day. Stage 1 is a behavioural detector with sensitivity 0.95 and specificity 0.99. Accounts it flags go to an independent stage-2 detector with sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.98.

(a) Compute the PPV of stage 1 alone. [5]
(b) Compute the PPV after stage 2 and the overall sensitivity of the two-stage system. [7]
(c) How many accounts are flagged per day by stage 1, and by stage 2? How many true compromises are found? [5]
(d) Explain the operational value of the second stage in terms of analyst workload. [3]

Show full solution

(a) $p=0.0002$. True-positive mass $0.95\times0.0002=0.00019$; false-positive mass $0.01\times0.9998=0.009998$. $\text{PPV}_1=0.00019/0.010188=\mathbf{1.9\%}$ — 98 of every 100 alerts are false.

(b) The stage-2 prior is the stage-1 PPV, 0.01865. TP $=0.90\times0.01865=0.016785$; FP $=0.02\times0.98135=0.019627$. $\text{PPV}_2=0.016785/0.036412=\mathbf{46.1\%}$. Overall sensitivity $=0.95\times0.90=\mathbf{85.5\%}$ (assuming the stages' errors are independent).

(c) Compromised accounts per day: $50{,}000\times0.0002=10$. Stage-1 alerts: $50{,}000\times0.010188\approx509$. Stage-2 alerts: $509\times0.036412\approx18.5$. True compromises found: $10\times0.855\approx8.6$; check $18.5\times0.461\approx8.6$.

(d) Analysts review about 19 cases instead of about 509 per day, and roughly half of them are real rather than 2%. This cuts alarm fatigue at the cost of missing about 1.5 compromises per day, which must be weighed against the cost of a missed insider.

Problem 4: Regression to the mean and confounding 20 marks

(a) Readiness scores have population mean 70 and test–retest reliability $\rho=0.6$. The 10% of battalions with the lowest scores (mean 50) receive a new AI tutoring aid and score 62 on average at the next assessment. A control group of equally low-scoring battalions without the aid averages 57. Using the regression-to-the-mean model, how much improvement is expected without any intervention? Estimate the effect of the aid and comment. [10]
(b) Across units, simulator hours (X) correlate with exercise score (Y) at $r_{XY}=0.60$. Unit budget (Z) correlates $r_{XZ}=0.70$ and $r_{YZ}=0.75$. Compute the partial correlation $r_{XY\cdot Z}$ and interpret it. [6]
(c) Propose a design that would establish whether simulator hours causally improve scores. [4]

Show full solution

(a) Expected next score with no intervention: $\mu+\rho(\bar x-\mu)=70+0.6(50-70)=58$, an improvement of 8 points from regression to the mean alone. The control group (57, +7) agrees closely with this. The treated group's +12 includes about 7–8 points of regression, so the aid's estimated effect is $62-57=\mathbf{5}$ points, not 12. Claiming 12 would have tripled the apparent benefit.

(b) $r_{XY\cdot Z}=\dfrac{0.60-0.70(0.75)}{\sqrt{(1-0.49)(1-0.5625)}}=\dfrac{0.075}{\sqrt{0.2231}}=\dfrac{0.075}{0.4724}=\mathbf{0.16}$. Most of the raw association reflects budget: well-funded units both use simulators more and score higher. A weak residual link remains, which may be causal or due to other confounders.

(c) A randomised design: within budget strata, randomly assign comparable units to additional simulator hours or to the current allocation; measure scores blind to assignment, and pre-register the outcome and analysis. Randomisation balances budget and unmeasured confounders, and the difference in mean scores then estimates the causal effect.

Problem 5: Distribution shift and evaluation design 20 marks

A soil-moisture feature was measured for 8 training sites and 8 deployment sites:
Training: 0.10, 0.12, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20, 0.22, 0.25, 0.30
Deployment: 0.20, 0.26, 0.31, 0.35, 0.38, 0.40, 0.42, 0.45

(a) Compute the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic $D=\max_x|F_{\text{train}}(x)-F_{\text{deploy}}(x)|$. [6]
(b) Using the 5% critical value $1.36\sqrt{(n+m)/(nm)}$, decide whether the distributions differ. [3]
(c) A deep model scores 97% on training-domain test data. Design a pre-deployment evaluation protocol of at least five steps that would catch an Afghan-to-Iraq style failure, and state what result would block deployment. [11]

Show full solution

(a) Evaluate both empirical CDFs at each data point. At $x=0.25$: $F_t=7/8=0.875$, $F_d=1/8=0.125$, difference 0.75. At $x=0.30$: $F_t=1.000$, $F_d=2/8=0.25$, difference 0.75. All other points give smaller differences (e.g. $x=0.22$: 0.625). $\mathbf{D=0.75}$.

(b) Critical value $=1.36\sqrt{16/64}=0.68$. Since $D=0.75>0.68$, reject equality at the 5% level: the deployment sites are systematically wetter, so the training data do not represent the deployment domain.

(c) A model answer covers: (1) collect a labelled sample from the deployment domain (at least 100–200 cases, all expected terrains, seasons and threat variants) before fielding; (2) run KS or similar shift tests on every key feature, with a pre-set threshold; (3) report per-class recall on the deployment sample, especially the threat class, with confidence intervals, not only overall accuracy; (4) compare against a simple baseline and the training-domain score, and treat a drop of more than a set margin (e.g. 5 points) as a failure; (5) stress-test with adversarial and degraded inputs (sensor noise, camouflage); (6) pilot in advisory mode with human confirmation and log every disagreement; (7) re-train or apply domain adaptation if a test fails. Blocking rule: deployment-domain threat recall below the operational requirement, or a significant shift on safety-critical features without a successful domain-adaptation re-test, halts fielding.
