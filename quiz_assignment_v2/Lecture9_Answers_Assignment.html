Q1. You have unlabelled SIGINT intercepts and want to find recurring emitter types. The first method to try is:

  • A. Random forest
  • B. DQN
  • C. k-Means/GMM clustering (with DBSCAN for noise)
  • D. Logistic regression
Answer C. No labels and a grouping goal: unsupervised clustering.

Q2. Labelled radar tracks, tabular features, rare bomber class: a strong default is:

  • A. Naive Bayes
  • B. PPO
  • C. k-Means
  • D. Random forest or XGBoost with class weights, evaluated by per-class recall
Answer D. L2: RF 97.5%; boosting gave the best bomber recall.

Q3. A drone must output continuous thrust: choose:

  • A. DQN
  • B. PPO or SAC with a Gaussian policy
  • C. Ward clustering
  • D. Ridge regression
Answer B. Continuous actions call for policy-gradient methods.

Q4. Six cooperating units with intermittent comms: the default method is:

  • A. MAPPO under CTDE
  • B. IQL
  • C. Single-agent DQN
  • D. Lasso
Answer A. Centralised training, decentralised execution; MAPPO 82.4%.

Q5. Which formula gives the probability that an alarm is real?

  • A. \(TP/(TP+FN)\)
  • B. \(1-(1-\alpha)^m\)
  • C. \(sp/(sp+(1-c)(1-p))\)
  • D. \(2/\|w\|\)
Answer C. Positive predictive value (Bayes), dominated by the base rate p.

Q6. The scalar Kalman gain \(P/(P+R)\) is near 1 when:

  • A. The sensor is very noisy
  • B. The prediction is very uncertain relative to the sensor
  • C. Both are equal
  • D. There is no measurement
Answer B. Trust the measurement when it is more precise than the prediction.

Q7. Silhouette 0.712 with ARI 0.847 between k-Means and Ward indicates:

  • A. Random clusters
  • B. Overfitting
  • C. Strong, method-independent cluster structure
  • D. Too many features
Answer C. Convergent validity across methods.

Q8. Why is a 3-tier IF to AE to analyst design used?

  • A. Cheap screening, then expensive confirmation, keeps compute and false alerts manageable
  • B. It raises accuracy above any single model
  • C. It avoids training
  • D. It needs no thresholds
Answer A. Serial tiers trade a little sensitivity for far fewer false alarms.

Q9. Which DQN component prevents over-estimation?

  • A. Replay buffer
  • B. Dueling head
  • C. Epsilon decay
  • D. Double DQN's split of selection and evaluation
Answer D. Max over noisy estimates is biased upward.

Q10. PPO's clipping keeps:

  • A. Rewards bounded
  • B. The critic fixed
  • C. Each policy update close to the previous policy
  • D. Exploration constant
Answer C. Trust-region behaviour without second-order optimisation.

Q11. COMA solves which MARL problem?

  • A. Non-stationarity
  • B. Credit assignment (free-riding)
  • C. Partial observability
  • D. Communication
Answer B. Counterfactual baselines credit each agent's marginal contribution.

Q12. AI-equipped units win 62% overall vs 80%, yet win more in every terrain. This is:

  • A. Simpson's paradox caused by unequal assignment to hard terrain
  • B. Regression to the mean
  • C. The base-rate fallacy
  • D. Overfitting
Answer A. Compute totals from counts and stratify by the confounder.

Q13. A model reaches 99% in simulation and 80% in the field. The most likely cause is:

  • A. Label noise
  • B. A bug in the metric
  • C. Too few parameters
  • D. Distribution shift / sim-to-real gap
Answer D. Address with domain randomisation and deployment-domain testing.

Q14. For lethal engagements the course recommends:

  • A. Level 4 autonomy
  • B. Level 2: AI recommends, humans approve each action, with audit logs
  • C. Level 3 if accuracy exceeds 95%
  • D. No logging
Answer B. IHL distinction and proportionality need accountable human judgement.

Q15. An answer stating 'the AI always beats humans' should prompt you to check:

  • A. Nothing
  • B. The colour of the charts
  • C. Only the learning rate
  • D. Sample size, confidence intervals, simulation conditions and opponent adaptivity
Answer D. Be suspicious of absolutes; MAPPO 82.4% vs 81.3% is not a demonstrated difference.

Part B — Assignment

Instructions. Four integrative problems, 100 marks, about 3 hours. Each draws on several lectures; answers must cite numbers and state limitations.

Problem 1: End-to-end ISR pipeline 30 marks

A theatre ISR cell receives radar tracks (labelled history available), SIGINT intercepts (unlabelled), network logs, and wants a decision-support tool for allocating two ISR drones.

(a) Map each data stream to a learning paradigm and algorithm, with justification. [10]
(b) Identify three Lecture-1 data pitfalls likely in these streams and how you would test for each. [9]
(c) Specify how outputs reach the commander, with autonomy level and audit. [6]
(d) Estimate the end-to-end latency budget. [5]

Show full solution

(a) Radar: supervised classification (RF/XGBoost, class weights, per-class recall). SIGINT: clustering (k-Means/GMM + DBSCAN noise for novel emitters) and Isolation Forest for zero-day signals. Network logs: tiered anomaly detection (IF → autoencoder → analyst; graph methods for lateral movement). Drone allocation: RL (PPO/SAC for continuous control; MAPPO for coordinating two drones), trained in simulation with domain randomisation.

(b) Survivorship/selection bias in labelled tracks (only engagements that were recorded) — compare label sources and coverage; distribution shift from exercise to theatre — KS tests on features and deployment-domain validation; base-rate effects in anomaly alerts — compute precision at the operational base rate. Also possible: confounding in effectiveness analyses (Simpson) — stratify.

(c) A fused common picture with threat classes, calibrated confidences and explanations; ranked anomaly alerts; drone-tasking recommendations (top-3 with values) requiring commander approval (Level 2); logs of inputs, recommendations, decisions and outcomes.

(d) Edge inference 10–100 ms; fusion and decision support 0.1–1 s; human decision 1–10 s: roughly 1.5–11 s sensor-to-action, which is acceptable for ISR tasking but not for terminal air defence.

Problem 2: Reading an evaluation report critically 25 marks

A vendor reports: ``Our threat detector is 99.2% accurate (tested on 2,000 balanced samples) and outperforms the incumbent (98.9%). In the field it will cut missed threats dramatically.'' The operational stream contains 1 threat per 5,000 tracks.

(a) Compute the 95% confidence interval for 99.2% on 2,000 samples, and comment on the comparison. [6]
(b) Assuming sensitivity and specificity of 99.2%, compute the PPV in the field. [7]
(c) List four questions you would put to the vendor. [8]
(d) Write a one-paragraph recommendation. [4]

Show full solution

(a) SE $=\sqrt{0.992(0.008)/2000}=0.0020$; CI $99.2\pm0.4\%=(98.8,99.6)$. The incumbent's 98.9% lies inside: no demonstrated superiority; a paired (McNemar) test on the same samples is needed.

(b) $p=0.0002$: PPV $=0.992(0.0002)/(0.992(0.0002)+0.008(0.9998))=0.000198/0.008197=\mathbf{2.4\%}$. About 41 false alarms per true threat.

(c) Per-class recall on the threat class and its CI; performance on data from our theatre (shift); how test data were collected (survivorship, duplicates, leakage); calibration and threshold choice; adversarial robustness; latency; results across repeated trials.

(d) Do not accept the claim as stated: the accuracy advantage is within sampling error and field precision would be about 2%. Request deployment-domain evaluation at the operational base rate, threat-class recall with confidence intervals, and a two-stage alert design before procurement.

Problem 3: From simulation to fielded autonomy 25 marks

A MARL team policy scores 88% in simulation (CommsMAPPO). Analogous drone results fell from 94% to 78% without domain randomisation and to 88% with it.

(a) Estimate a plausible field range for the MARL policy and justify. [6]
(b) Identify three ways an adaptive adversary could exploit the policy. [9]
(c) Propose a validation and governance plan, including an autonomy level. [10]

Show full solution

(a) Applying the drone gaps: about 72% without randomisation (a 16-point drop), about 82% with it (a 6-point drop); multi-agent coordination, communications denial and human OPFOR may widen this, so plan for a 65–80% range until field data exist.

(b) Spoofing or jamming the learned message channel; predictable sequencing (e.g. SEAD-first) baited by decoy SAMs; exploiting reward artefacts (e.g. reluctance to fire) or out-of-distribution scenarios.

(c) Domain randomisation; league training against diverse opponents; red-teaming; staged trials (simulation → hardware-in-the-loop → live) with pre-set acceptance thresholds and CIs; probing and securing messages; Level-2 operation with human approval of engagements; audit logging; monitoring and periodic re-validation.

Problem 4: Statistical traps in an after-action report 20 marks

An after-action report claims: (i) battalions given a new simulator after the worst scores of the year improved by 11 points; (ii) of 20 tactics tested, tactic 7 was significant ($p=0.03$); (iii) catering quality correlates with win rate ($r=0.71$).

For each claim, name the trap, explain it, and propose a correct analysis. [20]

Show full solution

(i) Regression to the mean: units selected for extreme low scores tend to improve anyway. Compare with a control group of equally low-scoring units without the simulator (or randomise); estimate the effect as the difference in improvement.

(ii) Multiple testing: with 20 tests, $P(\ge1$ false positive$)=64\%$. Apply Bonferroni ($0.05/20=0.0025$; 0.03 fails) and confirm in a fresh, pre-registered test.

(iii) Confounding: budget/readiness drives both. Compute the partial correlation controlling for budget (it fell to 0.09 in the course example) or use a controlled design; correlation is a question, not an answer.
