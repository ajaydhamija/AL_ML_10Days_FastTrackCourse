Q1. Reinforcement learning differs from supervised learning mainly because:

  • A. The agent learns from rewards generated by its own interaction, with delayed and sparse feedback, not labelled answers
  • B. It uses neural networks
  • C. It needs no data
  • D. It is unsupervised clustering
Answer A. No one labels the correct move; the agent discovers it from consequences.

Q2. An MDP is defined by:

  • A. (S, A, P, R, gamma)
  • B. (X, Y, loss)
  • C. (k, epsilon, MinPts)
  • D. (Q, K, V)
Answer A. States, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, discount factor.

Q3. With gamma = 0.95 the effective planning horizon is roughly:

  • A. 5 steps
  • B. 95 steps
  • C. 20 steps
  • D. Infinite
Answer C. \(1/(1-\gamma)=20\).

Q4. If OPFOR positions are hidden by fog of war, the problem is best modelled as:

  • A. A bandit
  • B. A supervised task
  • C. A POMDP: the agent must act on beliefs or memory
  • D. A deterministic MDP
Answer C. The Markov property fails for observations; real warfare is partially observable.

Q5. The Bellman optimality equation for Q* states that Q*(s,a) equals:

  • A. Expected immediate reward plus discounted value of the best next action
  • B. The immediate reward only
  • C. The average of all Q values
  • D. The probability of winning
Answer A. \(Q^*=\sum_{s'}P[R+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')]\).

Q6. Q(s, attack) = 18.7 means:

  • A. An 18.7% chance of winning
  • B. Expected discounted cumulative reward of attacking now and acting optimally afterwards
  • C. 18.7 enemy units destroyed
  • D. The learning rate
Answer B. Q-values are in reward units, not probabilities.

Q7. Q = 8, r = 2, max Q(s') = 10, gamma = 0.9, alpha = 0.5. The updated Q is:

  • A. 9.0
  • B. 11.0
  • C. 9.5
  • D. 10.0
Answer C. Target 11, TD error 3, new Q = 8 + 0.5 x 3 = 9.5.

Q8. Q-learning is called off-policy because:

  • A. It needs a model
  • B. It uses a target network
  • C. It never explores
  • D. Its target uses the greedy max action, not necessarily the action the exploring agent takes next
Answer D. SARSA, by contrast, uses the action actually taken (on-policy).

Q9. Why must epsilon never reach zero during training?

  • A. It slows computation
  • B. Untried actions would never have their values corrected
  • C. It causes overestimation
  • D. The buffer would overflow
Answer B. Convergence requires visiting all state-action pairs; keep some exploration (e.g. 0.05).

Q10. Experience replay helps DQN by:

  • A. Only saving memory
  • B. Breaking temporal correlation between consecutive samples and reusing data
  • C. Removing the need for rewards
  • D. Making Q-values probabilities
Answer B. Both stability and sample efficiency.

Q11. The target network's role is to:

  • A. Explore
  • B. Provide stable targets by freezing a copy of the weights for C steps
  • C. Pick actions
  • D. Shape rewards
Answer B. Without it the target moves with every update.

Q12. Standard DQN over-estimates values because:

  • A. Rewards are biased
  • B. Gamma is too small
  • C. The max over noisy estimates preferentially picks positive noise
  • D. Replay is random
Answer C. Double DQN decouples selection (online) from evaluation (target): 18.4 to 4.2 units.

Q13. The dueling architecture decomposes Q into:

  • A. Mean and variance
  • B. Actor and critic
  • C. Reward and penalty
  • D. State value V(s) and advantage A(s,a), minus the mean advantage
Answer D. \(Q=V+A-\bar A\); helps when many actions are equally good.

Q14. Potential-based reward shaping \(F=\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)\) is safe because:

  • A. It is always positive
  • B. It only affects terminal states
  • C. It removes the discount
  • D. Shaping terms telescope to a constant, so the optimal policy is unchanged
Answer D. Ng, Harada & Russell (1999).

Q15. Curriculum learning raised the final win rate from 51% (direct) to 91% because:

  • A. Easier early tasks give dense learning signal whose knowledge transfers to harder tasks
  • B. It used a bigger network
  • C. It changed the reward
  • D. It removed exploration
Answer A. Drills before full exercises.

Part B — Assignment

Instructions. Five problems, 100 marks, plus a 20-mark bonus problem (Problem 6). Reference: Lecture 6 reference text.

Problem 1: Discounting and tactical preference 15 marks

Policy A earns +10 one step from now; policy B earns +25 ten steps from now (rewards counted at step $k$ are weighted $\gamma^{k-1}$).

(a) Which is preferred with $\gamma=0.9$ and with $\gamma=0.95$? [8]
(b) Find the $\gamma$ at which the agent is indifferent, and interpret. [7]

Show full solution

(a) $\gamma=0.9$: A $=10$; B $=0.9^9\times25=0.387\times25=9.69$: A preferred. $\gamma=0.95$: B $=0.630\times25=15.8$: B preferred.

(b) $\gamma^9=0.4\Rightarrow\gamma=0.4^{1/9}=\mathbf{0.903}$. The discount factor encodes how far ahead the agent plans; a small change flips a quick limited gain into a patient larger one. $\gamma$ is a command decision about time preference, not a mere tuning knob.

Problem 2: Solving a small MDP 20 marks

From Safe the agent can advance to Contact (reward 0) or hold (stay, reward 0). From Contact, attack succeeds with probability 0.6 (reward +10, episode ends) or fails with 0.4 (reward $-4$, back to Safe); withdraw returns to Safe with reward 0. $\gamma=0.9$.

(a) Write the Bellman optimality equations. [6]
(b) Solve for $V^*(\text{Safe})$ and $V^*(\text{Contact})$, assuming advance and attack are optimal, and verify that assumption. [10]
(c) How would the optimal policy change if failure cost $-30$? [4]

Show full solution

(a) $V(S)=\max\{0.9V(C),\,0.9V(S)\}$; $V(C)=\max\{0.6(10)+0.4(-4+0.9V(S)),\;0.9V(S)\}$.

(b) With advance and attack: $V(S)=0.9V(C)$ and $V(C)=6-1.6+0.36V(S)=4.4+0.324V(C)$, so $V(C)=4.4/0.676=\mathbf{6.51}$ and $V(S)=\mathbf{5.86}$. Check: withdraw gives $0.9\times5.86=5.27<6.51$; hold gives $0.9\times5.86<0.9\times6.51$. Assumption confirmed.

(c) Attack value $=6+0.4(-30+0.9V(S))=-6+0.36V(S)$. If attacking, $V(C)=-6+0.324V(C)$ gives $V(C)<0$, worse than withdrawing (value $\ge0$). The optimal policy becomes never to attack (holding or withdrawing, value 0): a high enough failure cost makes the objective not worth the risk.

Problem 3: Tracing Q-learning 20 marks

Corridor cells 1–2–3; entering cell 3 gives +10 and ends the episode; every other move gives $-1$. $\gamma=0.9$, $\alpha=0.5$, all $Q=0$. The agent moves right every time.

(a) Compute $Q(1,R)$ and $Q(2,R)$ after episodes 1, 2 and 3. [12]
(b) What are the true values $Q^*$? Why does learning propagate backward one cell per episode? [8]

Show full solution

(a) Ep 1: $Q(1,R)=0.5(-1+0)=-0.5$; $Q(2,R)=0.5(10)=5$. Ep 2: $Q(1,R)=-0.5+0.5(-1+4.5+0.5)=\mathbf{1.5}$; $Q(2,R)=5+0.5(5)=\mathbf{7.5}$. Ep 3: $Q(1,R)=1.5+0.5(-1+6.75-1.5)=\mathbf{3.625}$; $Q(2,R)=\mathbf{8.75}$.

(b) $Q^*(2,R)=10$; $Q^*(1,R)=-1+0.9\times10=8$. Each update looks only one step ahead, so information about the goal reaches cell 1 only after cell 2's estimate has grown. With sparse rewards over 40 turns this is very slow: multi-step targets, reward shaping and curricula speed propagation.

Problem 4: Over-estimation and DQN design 20 marks

(a) Seven actions all have true value 5; each estimate has independent noise uniform on $[-1,1]$. Using $E[\max]=(n-1)/(n+1)$ for $n$ such variables, compute the expected over-estimation of $\max_a\hat Q$. Repeat for 3 actions. [6]
(b) Explain how Double DQN removes most of this bias. [6]
(c) Specify a DQN for the wargame: input encoding, architecture, loss, replay, target updates, exploration, and evaluation protocol. [8]

Show full solution

(a) $n=7$: $6/8=\mathbf{0.75}$ units of upward bias; $n=3$: $\mathbf{0.5}$. Bias grows with the number of actions, and bootstrapping compounds it through the targets.

(b) The online network chooses $a^*=\arg\max Q(s',\cdot;\theta)$, and the target network, whose errors are largely independent, evaluates $Q(s',a^*;\theta^-)$. Selection noise no longer inflates the evaluated value. In the case study the bias fell from 18.4 to 4.2.

(c) A model answer: encode hex contents and unit attributes (410 inputs, or a CNN on the grid); two 512-unit ReLU layers; dueling head with 7 outputs; Huber loss; Adam at $10^{-4}$; 100K replay with batch 32 (optionally prioritised); target copy every 1,000 steps; Double-DQN targets; $\varepsilon$ decayed from 1.0 to 0.05; curriculum from small maps. Evaluate against random, scripted, minimax and human opponents over many seeds, reporting means with confidence intervals; test for exploitability with an adaptive opponent.

Problem 5: Reward shaping and reward hacking 25 marks

Potential $\Phi(s)=-d(s)$, where $d$ is the distance in hexes to the objective (0 at the objective); $\gamma=0.9$; base reward 0 for moves.

(a) Compute the shaping reward $F=\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)$ for a move from $d=5$ to $d=4$, from $d=4$ to $d=5$, and for staying at $d=4$. [9]
(b) Staying gives a positive shaping reward. Show by telescoping that this cannot make loitering optimal. [8]
(c) A different designer rewards +1 for every move that reduces distance. Explain how the agent could exploit this and how to fix it. [8]

Show full solution

(a) $5\to4$: $0.9(-4)+5=\mathbf{1.4}$. $4\to5$: $0.9(-5)+4=\mathbf{-0.5}$. Stay at 4: $0.9(-4)+4=\mathbf{0.4}$.

(b) Over any trajectory, $\sum_t\gamma^tF_t=\gamma^T\Phi(s_T)-\Phi(s_0)$. For a trajectory that loiters forever this tends to $-\Phi(s_0)=d(s_0)$; for one that reaches the objective ($\Phi=0$) it equals $d(s_0)$ too. Every policy's return is shifted by the same constant $d(s_0)$, so the ranking of policies — and the optimal policy — is unchanged. Loitering earns shaping reward but forgoes the real objective reward.

(c) The agent can oscillate (step away, then back) collecting +1 on every approach without ever capturing the objective: reward hacking. Fix it by using potential-based shaping (stepping away then costs exactly what the return step gives back), capping or annealing shaping rewards, and auditing replays for degenerate behaviour.

Problem 6 (bonus): The tempting shortcut 20 marks

A unit in corridor cell S0 can move Right through S1 and S2 to the objective (each move costs $-1$; entering the objective from S2 gives $+20$ and ends the episode), or Left (stays in S0, cost $-1$). From S0 only, it can also Dash along an exposed road: with probability 0.6 it reaches the objective ($+20$), otherwise it is ambushed ($-30$); either way the episode ends. $\gamma=0.9$.

(a) Compute $V^*$ for S2, S1 and S0 and the value of Dash. Which action is optimal in S0? [5]
(b) Above what success probability $p$ would Dash become optimal? [3]
(c) Run three synchronous value-iteration sweeps from $V=0$ and show how S0's preferred action changes. [5]
(d) Q-learning with $\alpha=0.5$, all $Q=0$, experiences in order: S0 Right $\to$ S1 ($-1$); S1 Right $\to$ S2 ($-1$); S2 Right $\to$ objective ($+20$); S0 Dash $\to$ ambush ($-30$); S0 Right $\to$ S1 ($-1$); S1 Right $\to$ S2 ($-1$). Give every updated value. [5]
(e) With $\varepsilon=0.2$ over three actions in S0, what is the probability of choosing the greedy action? [2]

Show full solution

(a) $V^*(\text{S2})=20$; $V^*(\text{S1})=-1+0.9(20)=17$; Right from S0: $-1+0.9(17)=\mathbf{14.3}$. Dash: $0.6(20)+0.4(-30)=\mathbf{0}$. Right is optimal by a wide margin.

(b) $20p-30(1-p)=14.3\Rightarrow50p=44.3\Rightarrow p=\mathbf{0.886}$: the shortcut needs better than 88.6% odds.

(c) Sweep 1: S2 $=20$, S1 $=-1$, S0 $=\max(\text{Dash }0,\text{ Right }-1)=0$ (Dash preferred). Sweep 2: S1 $=17$; S0 still 0 (Right $=-1+0.9(-1)=-1.9$). Sweep 3: S0 $=-1+0.9(17)=14.3$ (Right preferred). Early in planning the immediate gamble looks best, because the value of the safe route has not yet propagated back — the same reason a half-trained agent can prefer risky shortcuts.

(d) $Q(\text{S0,R})=-0.5$; $Q(\text{S1,R})=-0.5$; $Q(\text{S2,R})=10$; $Q(\text{S0,D})=-15$; $Q(\text{S0,R})=-0.5+0.5(-1+0.9\cdot\max Q(\text{S1})-(-0.5))=-0.75$ (S1's best value was still 0, from an untried Left); $Q(\text{S1,R})=-0.5+0.5(-1+0.9\times10+0.5)=\mathbf{3.75}$.

(e) $0.8+0.2/3=\mathbf{0.867}$; each other action has probability $0.067$.
